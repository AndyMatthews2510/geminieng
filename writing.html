Shift the Mental Model: Lab Report vs. Journal Article

Undergraduates default to chronological storytelling ("First we did X, then we measured Y"). Published journal articles are structured as rhetorical arguments, not diaries. The primary objective is establishing an original technical contribution to international peers.

Dimension Coursework / Lab Report Engineering Journal Article (IEEE, ASME, Elsevier)
Target Audience Course instructor assessing compliance and correct execution. International researchers and practitioners seeking reproducible insights.
Primary Objective Demonstrate understanding of taught procedures to earn a grade. Prove an original methodological, structural, or analytical contribution.
Narrative Arc Chronological log of classroom steps taken. Problem-solution argument organized by technical significance.
Handling Flaws & Errors Often downplayed or blamed on generic "human error." Characterized using error propagation, confidence bounds, and physical mechanisms.

Dissecting the Anatomy of an Engineering Paper (IMRaD)

Title & Abstract: The Billboard

The abstract should strictly follow a 5-sentence arc:

Sentence 1 (Broad Context): Why does this broad engineering problem matter in industry or research?
↓
Sentence 2 (Specific Problem / Gap): What are the precise limitations of current state-of-the-art solutions?
↓
Sentence 3 (Proposed Method): What system, algorithm, or methodology was developed to overcome this gap?
↓
Sentence 4 (Quantitative Results): What were the specific, measured performance metrics (with units and bounds)?
↓
Sentence 5 (Engineering Consequence): What concrete impact does this outcome offer for practical implementation?

Introduction: The Funnel (CARS Model)

Follow John Swales' Create a Research Space (CARS) model:

  • Step 1 Establish the Territory: Provide engineering context and synthesize relevant, recent peer-reviewed literature.
  • Step 2 Establish the Niche: State the gap explicitly (e.g., "However, existing analytical models fail to account for non-linear damping above 100 Hz...").
  • Step 3 Occupy the Niche: State your explicit contribution (e.g., "In this paper, we propose and validate...") and summarize the paper's organization.

Methodology: Focus on Reproducibility

If an independent engineer cannot replicate the experimental setup or numerical simulation from your description, the section fails. Explicitly document instrument models, calibration tolerances (±), sample geometry standards (e.g., ASTM/ISO), software solver algorithms, and mesh convergence criteria.

Results vs. Discussion: Separate Observations from Physics

Results present objective, factual trends with direct references to figures and tables (e.g., "Figure 4 shows peak torque decreased by 12% above 3,000 RPM").
Discussion interprets the physical cause and compares findings to literature (e.g., "This reduction aligns with core loss models reported by Smith et al., likely driven by eddy current saturation in rotor laminations").

Common Technical Writing Traps for Undergraduates

  • Data Dumping Without Narrative: Presenting 10 graphs when 2 would suffice. Every figure must answer a specific engineering question raised in the narrative.
  • Passive vs. Active Voice Misuse: Modern engineering journals encourage direct active voice for author design decisions ("We designed a dual-stage filter...") and passive voice for standard physical procedures ("Samples were annealed at 400°C for two hours").
  • Imprecise Claims & Missing Error Bounds: Vague qualifiers ("runs very fast", "efficiency is high") must be replaced with bounded metrics ("latency decreased by 18.4% (±1.2%) compared to the baseline").
  • Neglecting Visual Standards: Figures must be high-DPI or vector formats, fonts must remain legible when scaled down, and all axes must specify both the physical quantity and standard SI unit in parentheses.

Recommended Scaffolded Writing Progression

Phase 1: Reverse-Engineering — Analyze 2 target papers from the intended journal; inspect heading structures, figure styles, and citation density.
↓
Phase 2: Figures & Captions First — Generate publication-quality plots and write standalone captions that convey test conditions and the main takeaway.
↓
Phase 3: Methods & Results Draft — Write the core empirical sections where data and parameters are clearest.
↓
Phase 4: Introduction & Discussion — Situate the work within the literature; interpret anomalies and state physical limitations.
↓
Phase 5: Abstract, Title, & Peer Review — Condense into the 5-sentence arc and conduct reciprocal peer reviews using the rubric.
